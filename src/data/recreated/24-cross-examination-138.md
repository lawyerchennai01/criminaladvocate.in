In a cheque bounce trial under **Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881**, the case often turns on the cross-examination of the complainant. The law starts with two **presumptions in the complainant's favour**: that the cheque was issued for a debt or liability (Section 139) and that a negotiable instrument was made for consideration (Section 118). The accused's task is to raise a **probable defence** that shakes these presumptions, and the cross-examination is the main tool for doing so. This page is for general understanding; actual cross-examination is a skill exercised by an advocate in court.

## How the trial is structured

- Section 138 cases follow a **summary trial** procedure (Section 143), intended to be quick.
- The complainant's **evidence is given by affidavit** (Section 145), and the accused can apply to **cross-examine** the complainant on it.
- After the complainant's evidence, the court records the **statement of the accused**, and the accused may lead evidence.

## The burden and the standard

The Supreme Court has held that the presumption under Section 139 includes the existence of a **legally enforceable debt** (*Rangappa v. Mohan*, 2010). The accused does not have to prove the defence beyond reasonable doubt. It is enough to show, on the **preponderance of probabilities**, that the existence of the debt is improbable, which can be done through the complainant's own evidence and documents (*Basalingappa v. Mudibasappa*, 2019). Even a **blank signed cheque** voluntarily handed over can attract the presumption (*Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar*, 2019).

## Areas commonly explored in cross-examination

1. **The alleged debt.** The date, the amount, the reason for the loan and the circumstances. Vague or shifting answers on these points can weaken the claim.
2. **Capacity to lend.** The complainant's source of funds, bank statements and income tax returns. In larger cash transactions, questions on compliance with income tax rules on cash loans may arise.
3. **Documents.** Whether any promissory note, receipt, ledger entry or message supports the loan, and whether the documents are consistent with the complaint.
4. **The cheque.** Who filled in the amount, date and name, and whether the handwriting differs from the signature. Whether the cheque was given as security.
5. **Presentation and dishonour.** The dates of presentation, the return memo and the reason for dishonour.
6. **The legal notice.** Whether the notice was sent within **thirty days** of receiving the bank memo, whether it demanded the correct amount and whether it was properly served.
7. **The complaint timeline.** Whether the complaint was filed within **one month** after the fifteen-day payment period.
8. **Authority of the person giving evidence.** If a power of attorney holder deposes, whether they have personal knowledge of the transaction (*A.C. Narayanan v. State of Maharashtra*, 2014).
9. **Inconsistencies** between the complaint, the legal notice, the affidavit and the oral evidence.

## Points to remember

- Cross-examination should be **precise and purposeful**, avoiding questions that allow the complainant to fill gaps in the case.
- The accused's own statement under the code of criminal procedure is not on oath, but the accused should not **make admissions** that harm the defence.
- A **rebuttal** can also come from the accused's evidence or documents, so the cross-examination should be planned with the defence evidence.
- If convicted, the accused who appeals must deposit **at least twenty per cent** of the compensation under Section 148, so planning the defence early is important.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the accused avoid cross-examination?
No. It is the accused's main opportunity to challenge the complainant's case.

### What if the accused admits signing the cheque?
Admitting the signature supports the presumption. The defence then focuses on showing there was no legally enforceable debt.

### Does the complainant need to show the loan in the income tax return?
It is a relevant factor in testing the complainant's case, though not conclusive by itself.

### How many questions can be asked?
There is no fixed number, but the court controls irrelevant or repetitive questions.

### Can the case be settled during the trial?
Yes. Section 147 allows the offence to be compounded.
