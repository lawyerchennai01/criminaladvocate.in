When two parties settle a dispute that led to an FIR, they can ask the High Court to **quash** the case on the basis of the compromise, even where the offence is *non-compoundable*. The High Court uses its inherent power, Section 482 of the CrPC (now Section 528 of the BNSS). The court does not accept every compromise: it examines the offence, the circumstances and whether the settlement is genuine and voluntary.

## When compromise-based quashing is considered

The Supreme Court has laid down the approach in cases such as *Gian Singh v. State of Punjab* (2012), *Narinder Singh v. State of Punjab* (2014) and *Parbatbhai Aahir v. State of Gujarat* (2017). In summary, the High Court considers whether:

- the dispute is **predominantly private or civil in nature**, such as a commercial, matrimonial or family dispute;
- the offence is **not heinous**, such as murder, rape or serious offences against society, which are not quashed on compromise;
- the offence does not involve **public interest**, such as corruption cases or serious economic offences affecting the public;
- the chances of conviction are **remote** and continuing the case would cause further hardship; and
- the parties have settled **voluntarily**, without pressure.

## What the compromise deed should contain

A compromise deed (or joint memo of compromise) is signed by the parties and usually filed with an affidavit. A typical format:

1. **Title and date:** "Compromise Deed" with place and date.
2. **Parties:** names, parentage, addresses and identification details of the complainant and the accused.
3. **Background:** the FIR number, police station, date, sections and the facts that led to the dispute, in neutral language.
4. **Terms of settlement:** what has been agreed. If payment is involved, the amount, mode and date; if the dispute is matrimonial, the terms on property, custody and maintenance.
5. **Statement of voluntariness:** that the compromise is without coercion, undue influence or inducement.
6. **No further claim:** that the complainant does not wish to pursue the case and has no objection to quashing.
7. **Undertakings:** that neither party will make further complaints on the same subject.
8. **Signatures and witnesses:** signed by both parties, with the signature of their advocates and independent witnesses.

The deed should be accompanied by a **supporting affidavit** from each party confirming its contents.

## How the High Court proceeds

- The petition under Section 528 BNSS is filed with the FIR, the compromise deed and affidavits.
- The court issues notice to the **Public Prosecutor**, who may oppose or consent.
- The court often directs the parties to appear **before the Registrar, a Magistrate or the court** so that the settlement can be verified in person.
- The court may impose conditions, such as payment of costs to a legal services authority or a charitable cause.
- If satisfied, the court passes an order quashing the FIR and all proceedings.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Relying on a compromise for an offence the court treats as **serious or against society**.
- A deed that is **vague about payment**, or about the fact that the complainant is not coerced.
- Failing to **disclose** earlier proceedings between the parties.
- Presenting a compromise **after conviction**, which requires a different approach.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a compromise automatically end a criminal case?
No. For non-compoundable offences, only the High Court (or Supreme Court) can quash the case after considering the settlement.

### Can the police oppose the quashing?
The State, through the Public Prosecutor, can oppose it and may point to the seriousness of the offence.

### Do both parties need to appear?
Typically yes, or at least the complainant must confirm the compromise before the court, often through an advocate and by personal appearance if directed.

### Is a notarised deed enough?
Notarisation helps but is not enough on its own. The court looks at the substance of the settlement and its voluntariness.

### Can a cheque bounce case be compromised?
Offences under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act can be compounded under Section 147 of that Act, usually before the trial court.
