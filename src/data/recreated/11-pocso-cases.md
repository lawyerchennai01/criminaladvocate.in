The **Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012** is a special law that protects every person below eighteen years from sexual assault, sexual harassment and pornography. It creates child-friendly procedures and **Special Courts**, and it contains strong presumptions against the accused. Because the stakes for both the child and the accused are high, understanding the process early helps families make informed decisions.

## Main offences under the Act

- **Penetrative sexual assault** (Sections 3 and 4), with a minimum imprisonment of ten years that may extend to life, and a higher minimum where the child is below sixteen.
- **Aggravated penetrative sexual assault** (Sections 5 and 6), with a minimum of twenty years that may extend to life or death in some cases.
- **Sexual assault** (Sections 7 and 8) and **aggravated sexual assault** (Sections 9 and 10).
- **Sexual harassment** (Sections 11 and 12).
- **Use of a child for pornography** and related offences (Sections 13 to 15).
- **Abetment and attempt** (Sections 16 to 18).

## Mandatory reporting

Section 19 requires any person who knows or apprehends that an offence has been committed to report it to the police or the Special Juvenile Police Unit. Section 21 punishes the failure to report. This is why schools, hospitals and institutions often report promptly.

## Procedure in the Special Court

1. **FIR and recording of the child's statement.** The police record the statement at the child's home or a place of the child's choice, ideally by a woman officer in plain clothes. The statement is also recorded before a Magistrate.
2. **Medical examination.** It is done with the consent of the child and parent or guardian.
3. **Special Court.** The trial takes place in camera, with the child's identity protected (Section 23). The court must record the child's evidence within thirty days of taking cognizance and aims to complete the trial within a year.
4. **Child-friendly examination.** Questions to the child are put through the court, not directly by the defence.
5. **Support person and compensation.** A support person can assist the child, and the court may order interim and final compensation.

## Presumptions against the accused

Sections 29 and 30 of the Act presume that the accused committed the offence and had the required culpable mental state, *unless the contrary is proved*. These presumptions apply once the prosecution establishes the foundational facts. They are rebuttable: the accused can show, for example, through inconsistencies, medical evidence or the surrounding circumstances, that the allegation is untrue.

## Age and consent

A child below eighteen cannot legally consent for the purposes of the Act. The child's age is established using school records, birth certificates or, if those are not available, a medical ageing test, following the procedure in the Juvenile Justice Act. Cases involving adolescents in relationships are treated with care by the courts, but a person above eighteen is generally prosecuted even where the relationship was consensual.

## Rights of the accused

An accused is entitled to be informed of the charge, to consult counsel, to seek bail, to cross-examine prosecution witnesses within the safeguards of the Act, and to have a fair trial. False implication, whether arising from family disputes, custody conflicts or other motives, is examined by the court on the evidence.

## Choosing counsel

POCSO cases require both legal skill and sensitivity. Questions worth asking an advocate are:

- Have they handled trials in the POCSO Special Court?
- How do they deal with the child's evidence in a respectful manner?
- Do they explain the presumptions and possible outcomes honestly, without guaranteeing a verdict?
- Is there a written fee agreement?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can bail be granted in a POCSO case?
Yes, depending on the allegations, the evidence and the circumstances. Courts consider the gravity of the offence, the child's safety and the risk of influencing witnesses.

### Can a POCSO case be quashed on compromise?
Courts are cautious, particularly where the child's interests are at stake. Each case turns on its facts.

### Is a medical examination compulsory?
It is an important evidence-gathering step but must be conducted with the child's consent and appropriate safeguards.

### How long does a POCSO trial take?
The Act aims for completion within a year, but delays occur in practice.

### Where can a child get help?
CHILDLINE 1098, the police emergency number 112 and the Child Welfare Committee are the main points of contact.
