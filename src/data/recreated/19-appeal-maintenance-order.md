An order granting or refusing maintenance under **Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**, which replaced Section 125 of the CrPC, can be challenged. The correct remedy depends on **which court passed the order** and whether the order is **final or interim**. This guide walks through the steps for a person who wishes to challenge an order, whether as the paying spouse or as the claimant.

## Step 1: Identify the order and the court

- **Final order of a Family Court.** An appeal lies to the **High Court** under Section 19 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, ordinarily **within thirty days** of the order.
- **Order of a Magistrate (where there is no Family Court).** A **revision** lies to the Sessions Court or the High Court under the revision provisions of the BNSS (Section 438 onwards).
- **Interim maintenance order.** The Family Courts Act does not allow an appeal against an interim order, so the usual route is a **revision** or a petition under the High Court's inherent power (Section 528 BNSS) in an appropriate case.

## Step 2: Check limitation

For a Family Court appeal, the period is thirty days from the date of the order. If you are late, apply to **condone the delay**, supported by an affidavit explaining the reason. Courts allow delay for good reason but do not do so automatically.

## Step 3: Obtain the records

Get a **certified copy** of the order, the petition, the counter, the evidence and any interim orders. Check what income, assets and expenses the court considered.

## Step 4: Identify the grounds

Typical grounds in a maintenance appeal include:

- the court **misjudged the husband's income** or ignored his liabilities;
- the court **ignored the wife's income or capacity to earn**, or other relevant material;
- the amount is **disproportionate** to the evidence;
- the court did not follow the Supreme Court's guidance requiring **disclosure of income and assets by affidavit** (*Rajnesh v. Neha*, 2020);
- there is a **legal bar**, for example where the claimant is living in adultery or where parties are living separately by mutual consent without sufficient reason;
- the order for **arrears** or the **date from which maintenance is payable** is wrong.

For a claimant who is dissatisfied, the grounds usually relate to an inadequate amount, the failure to consider lifestyle, or the date from which maintenance starts.

## Step 5: Draft and file the appeal or revision

Prepare the memorandum of appeal or the revision petition, with a list of dates, grounds and prayer. Attach the certified copy of the order, the key documents and an affidavit. File it in the High Court (or the Sessions Court for revision, where applicable) along with court fee and vakalatnama.

## Step 6: Ask for a stay if needed

If the order requires immediate payment, you can apply for **stay or suspension** of the order. Courts often require the appellant to **deposit a part of the arrears** or continue paying an interim amount as a condition. A stay is not automatic.

## Step 7: Follow the hearing and comply meanwhile

Until the High Court stays or modifies the order, it remains enforceable. Non-payment can lead to a **warrant for recovery** and, in some cases, imprisonment of up to one month for each month of default. Keep paying whatever is directed and keep proof of every payment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a maintenance order be modified later without an appeal?
Yes. If circumstances change, either party can apply to the same court to alter the allowance.

### Is there a time limit to file a revision?
Ninety days is generally taken as the period for a revision to the High Court, subject to condonation of delay.

### Will the High Court re-hear the whole evidence?
In an appeal, it can examine the record afresh. In revision, its review is more limited to legality and propriety.

### What if my spouse has hidden income?
The court can draw conclusions from lifestyle, bank records and the failure to disclose. The disclosure affidavit makes concealment risky.

### Can I appeal if maintenance was refused?
Yes, the claimant has the same right of appeal or revision.
