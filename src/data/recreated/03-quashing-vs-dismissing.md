**Quashing** means the High Court cancels a criminal proceeding, such as an FIR, complaint, charge sheet or the court case that follows, using its inherent powers. **Dismissal** is a term used for what a trial court or Magistrate does with a complaint or case, and it sits alongside related outcomes such as discharge, acquittal and closure. The two are not interchangeable: they come from different courts, on different grounds and with different consequences.

## Quashing

- **Who:** the High Court (and, in suitable cases, the Supreme Court under Article 142).
- **Power:** Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, now Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which saves the High Court's inherent power to prevent abuse of process or to secure the ends of justice. Article 226 of the Constitution is also used.
- **When:** the allegations, even if taken at face value, disclose no offence; the proceeding is a clear abuse of process or is mala fide; there is a legal bar; or the parties have genuinely settled a dispute that is mainly private in nature.
- **Effect:** the proceeding is wiped out at that stage. It ends the case without a trial, though the court can set conditions.

## Dismissal and similar trial-court outcomes

These are decisions of the trial court or Magistrate within the ordinary procedure:

- **Dismissal of a complaint.** After considering the complainant's statement and the material, a Magistrate can dismiss a private complaint if there is no sufficient ground to proceed (Section 203 CrPC, now carried into the BNSS).
- **Discharge.** Before charges are framed, the court can discharge the accused if the material does not disclose grounds to proceed (Sections 227, 239 and 245 CrPC).
- **Acquittal.** At the end of the trial, the court finds the accused not guilty, or the prosecution fails to prove its case beyond reasonable doubt.
- **Closure of the police report.** The police may file a final report that no offence is made out; the Magistrate can accept it or direct further investigation.
- **Withdrawal of prosecution.** The Public Prosecutor can seek the court's consent to withdraw (Section 321 CrPC).

## Key differences at a glance

| Point | Quashing | Dismissal, discharge or acquittal |
|---|---|---|
| Court | High Court | Trial court or Magistrate |
| Source of power | Inherent power and Article 226 | Procedure laid down in the CrPC / BNSS |
| Stage | Any stage, from FIR to trial | A defined stage of the trial |
| Appeal | Special Leave Petition to the Supreme Court | Appeal or revision as provided |
| Typical ground | Abuse of process, no offence made out, settlement | No sufficient ground, or prosecution fails on evidence |

## Does the same offence come back?

A quashed proceeding generally cannot be revived on the same facts, though the State can challenge the order before the Supreme Court. After a discharge or acquittal, the State may have a right of appeal or revision. After a dismissal of a complaint, the complainant may be able to file a fresh complaint in limited circumstances, such as new evidence.

## Choosing the right route

Which remedy is available depends on the stage and on what the record shows. An FIR that on its face discloses no offence is usually a quashing case. A weak charge sheet may be better challenged by a discharge application first, because the trial court can examine the material with the specific legal test. A genuine settlement of a private dispute may support a quashing petition or compounding, depending on the offence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an FIR be quashed before a charge sheet is filed?
Yes. The High Court can quash an FIR at the investigation stage, but it exercises the power sparingly and generally does not stop a lawful investigation.

### Is a discharge the same as an acquittal?
No. A discharge happens before charges are framed and is based on the material at the charge stage. An acquittal follows a trial or a finding of not guilty.

### Which is faster, quashing or discharge?
It depends on the court's list and the stage. Neither is guaranteed to be quick. The right choice should be made on the facts, not on speed.

### Can an offence like rape or murder be quashed on compromise?
Courts treat serious offences differently. Compromise is not accepted as a basis to quash heinous offences or offences against society. The High Court examines the nature of the offence and the facts.

### Do I need a lawyer to file a quashing petition?
A quashing petition is a High Court proceeding in which drafting and the correct legal grounds matter, so it is usually handled through an advocate.
