A cheque bounce case under **Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881** depends heavily on **strict timelines**. A missed deadline can end a complaint, so the lawyer you choose should be precise about dates and procedure. This page sets out the legal timeline and a practical checklist, without ranking any lawyer, since quality depends on the facts of your case.

## The statutory timeline

| Step | Time limit |
|---|---|
| Presenting the cheque to the bank | Within the validity of the cheque, ordinarily **three months** from the date on it |
| Sending the **legal notice** to the drawer | Within **30 days** of receiving the bank's return memo |
| Drawer's time to pay | **15 days** from receipt of the notice |
| Filing the **complaint** in court | Within **one month** after the 15 days expire |

The court can condone a delay in filing the complaint if sufficient cause is shown, but the notice period is strict.

## Where to file

Under Section 142(2), as amended in 2015, the complaint is filed in the court within whose jurisdiction the **payee's bank branch** where the cheque was deposited for collection is situated. For an account maintained in Chennai, the Metropolitan Magistrate courts in Chennai are the usual forum, though facts differ.

## Penalty and related powers

- Imprisonment up to **two years**, or a fine up to **twice the cheque amount**, or both.
- **Interim compensation** of up to twenty per cent of the cheque amount can be ordered at the start of the trial (Section 143A), and the accused who appeals a conviction must deposit at least twenty per cent (Section 148).
- The offence can be **compounded** by settlement at any stage (Section 147).

## Checklist for choosing a lawyer

1. **Experience in the right court.** Ask whether they regularly handle Section 138 trials and the relevant procedure in the Magistrate courts.
2. **Command of the timelines.** A good lawyer will tell you the deadlines for your case on the first meeting, in writing.
3. **Clear advice for your side.** The advice differs depending on whether you are the complainant or the accused.
4. **Document review.** They should ask for the cheque, the return memo, the notice, the postal proof and the underlying agreement.
5. **Honest about the outcome.** Avoid anyone who guarantees a result.
6. **Written fee agreement.** It should state the stages covered and any extra charges, and you should get receipts.
7. **Communication.** Do you get regular updates and copies of the filings?
8. **Settlement approach.** They should explain compounding and negotiation, not only litigation.

## If you are the complainant

- Keep the **original cheque and the bank return memo**.
- Send the notice to the **correct address** by a traceable method, and keep proof.
- Make sure the notice **demands the correct amount**.
- Be ready with proof of the **underlying transaction**.

## If you are the accused

- Do not ignore the notice. Reply within the time and explain your position.
- Preserve documents showing **payments made**, disputes or the **absence of a legally enforceable debt**.
- Consider a **settlement** early, since the case can be compounded.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file a cheque bounce case if the cheque was post-dated?
Yes, once the cheque is presented within its validity and is dishonoured, the process starts from the bank's return memo.

### Is a cheque dishonoured for "account closed" covered by Section 138?
Yes. Dishonour for insufficient funds or because the amount exceeds the arrangement are covered, and closed accounts are also generally treated as covered.

### Can a company be prosecuted?
Yes, along with persons in charge of its business at the time (Section 141).

### Can the case be filed online?
Some courts accept e-filing, which depends on the court's procedure. Ask your lawyer.

### How long does the case take?
It is meant to be summary, but it often takes longer than intended. See the cross-examination guide for how trials proceed.
